Nottingham Park Houses
The Park Conservation Trust — Historical Record

The Park, house by house

There is now a record of the estate — all 439 houses and the 8,352 people who lived in them between the first census in 1841 and the register taken in the first weeks of the war in 1939. It is free, it is open to anybody, and your house is almost certainly in it.

Find your house

On a house you will find when it was built and who built it, what the architects did to it since, photographs — 351 houses have one — and the households who lived there census by census: their ages, where they were born, and what they did for a living. 414 of the 439 houses have people in them, and 44 of them held twelve or more under one roof on a single census night — a family, and the people who worked for them.

 

It stops in 1939, and it holds nobody living.

The record is of people who have died. Nothing about anybody living is in it and nothing about anybody living will go in it — not who lives in a house now, not who owns one, not who is in a photograph. Where a house carries a name its owner uses today, the name goes in and the householder does not.

Photographs are of buildings, taken from the pavement. Never of people, never through a window, never over a wall.

If you think something on the site should not be there — about you or about anybody else — say so and it comes down while it is looked at. You do not have to explain why, and you do not need an account. Ask for something to be taken down.

Where it is wrong, we would like to know

A good deal of this was read off census pages written in pencil, in bad light, by a man walking door to door in an evening, and then typed up by somebody else a century later. Names are misspelt. Houses have been renumbered. Two houses have been made into one, and one house into four flats. If the record has your house wrong — or has it on the wrong street — it takes a minute to say so.

And the thing we most need is on your gatepost

Only 141 of the 439 houses have a name recorded. The rest have numbers and nothing else, though a great many of them carry a name on the gate, the gatepost, a fanlight or the first page of the deeds. That is the single largest gap in the record, and it is the one gap that cannot be closed in an archive — somebody has to stand in front of the house and read it.

If your house has a name, we would be glad of it. The same goes for old photographs, deeds, sale particulars, a conveyance with a plan on it, or anything your family remembers about who was here before you.

Tell us something

Or walk it

The record has a walk built into it. Tell it how long you have — fifteen minutes is enough — and it finds you, plots a route from where you are standing, and tells you about the houses as you reach them. One of the walks follows the enumerator's own round of the evening of 29 September 1939, door by door, in the order he knocked. It is not the order of the street: he doubles back, crosses over, and comes to the lodge last.

Walk it

If you would like to work on it properly

Anybody can. There is a guide to what every page is for, what you can do on it, and where to start if you have an hour — and the first thing in it is what can and cannot go in.
